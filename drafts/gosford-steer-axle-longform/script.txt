A tray truck is driving down the M1 Pacific Motorway at Mount White, north of Sydney. Nothing looks wrong with it. The driver has not done anything reckless. The company has a clean record going back to 2015.

Then it gets weighed.

The steer axle, the front axle that does the steering, is allowed to carry 6.5 tonnes. On this truck it is carrying 8.74 tonnes. That is 134.5 percent of the limit.

Under the Heavy Vehicle National Law, mass offences are graded by how far over you are. This one lands in the top band. The law calls it a severe risk breach.

On 22 September 2026, Gosford Local Court convicted the company and fined it $21,000. The maximum for that offence is $108,000. The court took the clean record into account, and said this was a first offence. It also said it was a big mistake, and that incorrect loading puts pressure on the road.

So how does a company with no prior offences end up with a severe risk breach?

The answer in the court record is one sentence. The load was placed too far forward on the truck tray.

That is the whole case. Nobody overloaded the truck as a whole. The weight was in the wrong place. Put a heavy load near the front of a tray and most of it sits over the steer axle. The back axles carry less, the front carries more, and the front is the axle with the smallest limit.

This is the part that catches operators out. A load can be under the total mass limit and still break the law, because every axle group has its own limit. The question is where the weight sits, as well as how much there is.

Why does the court care so much about a steer axle? Because that axle steers and brakes the truck. Overload it and the tyres run hotter, the steering gets heavier, and the stopping distance grows. The court's words were about pressure on the road, and the road is the public.

Let's look at what the regulator says operators should take from this. The NHVR published five points with the case. I will go through them, because together they make a Monday morning checklist.

One. Work out the mass limits that apply to each heavy vehicle, and tell the people who need to know. That means loaders and drivers, not just the office.

Two. Give those people a way to check the weight. On-board scales, portable scales, or a weighbridge on the route.

Three. Train everyone involved in mass, dimension and loading requirements. Not once, and not only the drivers.

Four. Build mass and load checks into the pre-start inspection checklist. If the checklist only covers lights and tyres, the load is nobody's job.

Five. Prepare load distribution plans and hand them to the loaders and drivers. A plan that says where the heavy items go, and why.

None of those five is expensive. Every one of them was missing, or did not work, on the day this truck left the yard.

Here is the no-blame question for your operation. Suppose a loader puts the heavy end of a load at the front of a tray tomorrow. Who notices before the truck reaches the motorway?

If the honest answer is nobody, the gap is in the system, and systems are what the court looks at.

One last point on the fine. $21,000 for a first offence with a clean record is about a fifth of the maximum. But the court said it needed to deter other operators, not just this one. A good record helps you. It does not make the loading plan optional.
